The Handoff Is the Product

A support bot that never escalates isn't efficient. It's unsupervised.

September 29, 2026 · 5 min read · research window 2024-02-01 to 2026-09-29

In November 2022, after his grandmother died, Jake Moffatt asked the chatbot on Air Canada's website about bereavement fares. It told him he could book now and apply for the discount retroactively. That wasn't Air Canada's policy, and when he applied, the airline refused. In the case that followed, Air Canada argued that the chatbot was responsible for its own statements. In February 2024 the British Columbia Civil Resolution Tribunal called that "a remarkable submission," found the airline liable for negligent misrepresentation, and ordered it to pay $812.02 CAD. The bot, the tribunal said, is still just part of Air Canada's website.

The dollar amount was trivial. The principle wasn't: when a bot states a policy, the company has stated a policy.

Escalation isn't what happens when the AI deployment fails. It's the part of the deployment that lets you ship it at all.

Before

Success is containment: the share of conversations resolved without a human.

After

Success is correct resolution, and a correct handoff counts as one.

Turn

A bot that never escalates isn't efficient. It's unsupervised.

Two weeks after the Air Canada ruling, Klarna published the most-cited containment story of the period. Its OpenAI-powered assistant had handled 2.3 million conversations in its first month, two-thirds of all customer service chats. Klarna said it did the work of 700 full-time agents, matched human satisfaction scores, and cut average resolution time from 11 minutes to under two. It became the slide in every AI customer-service pitch for a year.

In May 2025, Klarna's CEO, Sebastian Siemiatkowski, told Bloomberg the company was hiring human agents again. The cheaper AI-led service had come with lower quality, and Klarna wanted customers to always be able to reach a person. Nothing about the model had to break for that to happen. The metric it was managed to — how many conversations never reached a human — simply wasn't the thing customers valued.

Containment measures how often you avoided a person. Customers measure whether they got the right answer.

Before

Hallucination is a model-quality issue the vendor will fix.

After

A hallucinated policy is a commitment problem. Policy-shaped answers need a source or a human.

Turn

The bot doesn't need to be right about everything. It needs to know which statements the company will be held to.

In April 2025, Cursor showed how fast the same failure travels when the customers are developers. Users were getting logged out when they switched machines. One wrote to support and got a reply from "Sam" explaining that Cursor was designed to work on one device per subscription as a core security feature. There was no such policy, and Sam was an AI. The reply spread across Hacker News and Reddit, users threatened to cancel, and co-founder Michael Truell had to post publicly: "We have no such policy." The logouts turned out to be a side effect of a security change.

Look at what went wrong. Not the bug, which was ordinary. The bot invented a rule to explain it, users couldn't tell they were talking to a bot, and nothing stopped a policy-shaped answer from going out without a source. Each of those is a design decision an FDE makes, or leaves unmade, while scoping a support deployment.

Handoff coverage check

Which escalation triggers does your deployment actually implement?

Every FDE who has deployed a support bot has seen the dashboard that leads with containment rate, and the executive who wants the number higher. The useful thing to bring to that meeting is the cost of one wrong commitment: a tribunal ruling, a viral thread, a reversed hiring freeze. Then propose the metric that would have caught it: cost per correct resolution, with a weekly human audit of a sample of contained conversations.

The case against

The objection is that escalation is exactly the cost the customer bought AI to remove, and that a cautious bot hands off so often it's pointless. That's real if the triggers are vague ("escalate when unsure"). It isn't if they're narrow and concrete: policy-shaped claims without a source, money and account changes, an explicit request, repeated failure. Most support volume is order status, how-to questions and password resets, and none of those trip these triggers. The handoff rate stays low; the dangerous tail goes to people.

What this means for you

  • Inventory the commitments. List every kind of statement the bot could make that the company would be held to: refunds, fees, eligibility, deadlines, security rules.
  • Ground or hand off. Those statements come only from retrieved, versioned documents, cited in the answer. Otherwise route to a human.
  • Build the context packet. The human should never ask the customer to repeat themselves.
  • Disclose. Name the bot as AI in the first message and in email signatures.
  • Change the headline metric from containment to correct resolution, backed by a weekly audit sample.
24-hour action: Open your deployment's chat and ask for a refund exception, then ask for a human. Record what it said and how long it took. That transcript is your first audit sample.
