The Title Is the Price

March 14, 2025

A friend sent me two job postings last month. Same city. Same listed experience: three to six years. Same first bullet about working directly with customers to ship technical solutions. One read "Forward Deployed Software Engineer" and showed $205,000 total comp at median. The other read "Solutions Engineer" and showed $165,000. Palantir and Databricks, respectively, per Levels.fyi data reported in early March 2025. Forty thousand dollars separating two roles that both hiring managers would describe, if you asked them separately over coffee, as essentially the same Wednesday afternoon. The difference wasn't in the work. It was in who got blamed when the work went wrong — and that blame has a dollar sign attached to it.

Beat 1

BEFORE: You've been aggregating Levels.fyi data, polling Blind threads, building a spreadsheet — trying to nail down the "market rate" for FDE-adjacent roles so you can negotiate from a firm number.

AFTER: There is no single market rate. The title itself is the rate-setting mechanism.

TURN: The fragmentation you've been treating as a research problem is actually the answer.

Your title isn't describing your work. It's naming who absorbs the cost when an engagement goes sideways.

"Forward Deployed Software Engineer" at Palantir carries scoping authority and direct customer-outcome accountability baked into the role definition. When a deployment stalls — and deployments stall at a rate that would surprise anyone who hasn't lived it — that person owns the stall. Not their manager. Not a separate implementation team picked up in a handoff meeting two sprints later. Them. "Solutions Engineer" at Databricks often operates in a pre-sales-adjacent lane: the same technical demonstration and integration skills, the same customer-facing conversations, but with a post-sale handoff that means someone else inherits what happens after the contract signs. The $40,000 gap isn't paying for better engineering. It's paying for liability.

(I've held two of these title variants at different companies. The comp difference showed up in the offer letter. The Tuesday-morning work felt nearly identical. The 2am page was not.)

The Y Combinator Winter 2025 batch made the title-inflation problem starkly visible. Of roughly 740 engineering postings from that cohort, 22 carried "Forward Deployed Engineer" in the title — about 3%, up from well under 1% two years earlier. When someone on Hacker News traced eight of those listings back to their actual job descriptions and, where possible, conversations with the hiring managers, five turned out to be implementation and onboarding positions. No real scoping authority. No customer-outcome ownership. No "decide what we should actually build" moments. One of the five had a job description that was verbatim the same template as a "Senior Backend Engineer" posting from the same company, with the title swapped. The title was a recruiting signal aimed at engineers who'd read about Palantir FDEs and wanted that career — not a description of the work on offer. Show up expecting to own customer outcomes and you'd find yourself writing migration scripts from a prioritized Jira board.

A company that won't name the failure mode in your title won't let you own the fix when you find it.

The strongest pushback I'd expect: I'm overfitting to two companies, and Palantir simply pays more across the board, making the title correlation incidental. That argument doesn't survive the next data point. A FAANG L5 engineer last month compared an internal "Customer Solutions Engineer" lateral transfer at roughly $310,000 total comp against a Palantir FDE offer at $220,000. They took the internal move — the right call for their situation, because that FAANG role, despite carrying less title prestige in FDE-adjacent circles, included direct product-line P&L exposure that the Palantir mid-level FDE role didn't come close to touching. The scope variance within a single title family at one company outstripped the comp variance between two companies' entirely different title families. You cannot read risk allocation off a title by looking at comp numbers. You have to read the risk directly from the role's actual boundaries.

This dynamic deserves a name: title-as-risk-pricing. Companies are not confused about what to call these roles. They are making deliberate choices about which risks to attach to which headcount line, and the title is the contract that encodes that choice. "Field Engineer" at a Series B startup typically prices out at $130,000 to $170,000 total comp because the company is purchasing implementation capacityoutcome ownership — a person who can follow a deployment playbook reliably and hit the checklist items. The same person doing nominally similar technical work under "Forward Deployed Software Engineer" at a later-stage company costs more because that company is purchasing the right to hold someone accountable for a customer result. The Tuesday work looks alike. The risk allocation does not.

Beat 2

BEFORE: Optimize for the highest-TC title you can land — more money for similar work is strictly better.

AFTER: Optimize for the title that prices the risk you actually want to own.

TURN: The comp premium between titles isn't a reward for better work — it's hazard pay for more liability.

Within 24 hours: find one FDE-adjacent job posting that genuinely interests you. Rewrite its title to match what the description actually describes — implementation engineer, pre-sales technical, or outcome-owning deployer. The gap between the posted title and your rewrite is the information you've been missing.

What You'll Pick Up

A ~24% comp swing tied purely to which of four interchangeable titles a company picks
Over 60% of "FDE" titles at early-stage startups are mislabeled implementation roles
Within-family scope variance dwarfs cross-family comp variance — comparing across titles is a trap
