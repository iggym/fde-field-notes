Lost Before You Type

The invisible phase that decides every FDE engagement before an IDE opens.

In February 2025, Palantir reported $702 million in US commercial revenue for fiscal year 2024 — 54 percent higher than the year before. Their primary mechanism for winning those customers isn't a product demo or an ROI slide deck. It's a multi-week process they call a "bootcamp": forward-deployed engineers show up at a prospective customer's site, sit down with their actual data and their actual people, and start working. The FDEs sit with the customer's analysts, run their actual queries against their actual databases, and watch where the process breaks. No deployment contract exists yet. Nobody has committed to buying anything. The FDEs are there to figure out what's worth building — and what the customer initially asked for frequently turns out to be a symptom, not the problem. A logistics company asks for route optimization; two weeks of working alongside their dispatchers reveals the real bottleneck is a data pipeline feeding bad inputs into every downstream system. Only after the bootcamp ends does a commercial agreement get signed. On their Q4 earnings call, executives described this not as a sales technique but as engineering work: the scoping, the trust-building, and the assumption-killing that determines whether anything useful ever ships. The people doing discovery build the solution. No handoff. No translation layer between what sales promised and what engineering can deliver.

AWS Professional Services has run a parallel pattern for years through their Well-Architected Review — a structured assessment across six architectural pillars that typically surfaces 20 to 40 improvement opportunities per engagement. AWS has published extensive case studies positioning these reviews as the standard entry point for professional services engagements. The formal deliverable is a prioritized remediation list. The actual deliverable is harder to name but more important: both the AWS team and the customer team walk out of the review using the same words for the same problems. When a customer engineer says "we have a reliability gap" and the AWS engineer knows precisely what they mean — because they both just spent two weeks mapping concrete failure scenarios against the same six-pillar framework — that alignment is the product. That sharedvocabulary is what prevents the build phase from becoming a slow-motion collapse. Without it, you get the pattern every FDE has lived through: "performance issues" means five different things to five people on the same call, and nobody notices until sprint four, when you've already built the wrong optimization and the political cost of changing direction has tripled.

BEAT 2
The deliverable of discovery is a scoping document or technical brief.
The deliverable of discovery is shared mental models between your team and the customer's team.
The document is just the receipt proving the models aligned — it's evidence of the work, not the work itself.

The CNCF's 2024 State of Platform Engineering report, published in November, surfaced a related failure mode from the inside. Surveying hundreds of platform engineering practitioners about how they approached internal platform build-outs, the report found that teams which skipped formal discovery phases reported consistently higher rates of rework and abandoned features. The most common reason given for skipping wasn't time pressure or budget constraints. It was confidence: "we already know what our teams need." That stated confidence turned out to be a stronger predictor of failure than the technical complexity of the platform itself. Senior teams were just as vulnerable as junior ones. The harder the problem, the more dangerous the assumption that you understand it before you've looked.

Three different contexts — a vendor selling to enterprises, a cloud provider running assessments, internal platform teams building for their own organizations — and the same dynamic keeps appearing: the quality of the discovery phase determines the quality of everything that follows, and the organizations most confident they don't need discovery are the ones where its absence causes the most damage. The pattern is consistent enough to be useful: if someone on the engagement says "we don't need a discovery phase," that statement itself is the strongest signal that they do.

I've walked into customer sites with a clear mental model of what I was there to scope and walked out two weeks later with something completely different. That's not a miss — that's the job. If your discovery doesn't surprise you, you probably weren't paying attention.

The strongest counterargument is the one you'll hear in every procurement meeting: discovery costs real time and money upfront, and customers — especially those managing internal stakeholders who want deliverables — want to see momentum fast. The customer sees two weeks of "nothing to show for it." The alternative is two months of something that doesn't work. Palantir's numbers address this head-on. Their bootcamp-first model doesn't extend the overall engagement timeline — it compresses it. Build phases start with accurate scope instead of optimistic assumptions that unravel in month three. The two-week discovery investment prevents the two-month rework penalty. You pay now or you pay later, and later is always more expensive, because by then you're also paying for the political cost of having been wrong in front of the customer.
