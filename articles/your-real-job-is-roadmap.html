Your Real Job Is Roadmap

18 Feb 2025 Internal Advocacy Field Note #008

You're three weeks into a customer engagement when you notice it. The customer's data team is building something your product should already do — not as a hack, not as a one-off, but as a recurring pattern they've institutionalized. You've seen the same gap at two other accounts. You mention it on your internal Slack channel. Someone from product responds with a thumbs-up emoji. The message scrolls up and away. Six months later, a competitor ships the feature.

That thumbs-up emoji cost your company more than any bug you'll ever fix.

BEAT 1: Before → After The Advocacy Frame

Most FDEs describe their job as "making the product work at the customer." That's the deployment frame. It's honest and it's incomplete. The deployment frame treats the product as fixed and the customer as variable — your job is to bend the customer toward the product's existing shape.

But the actual highest-leverage work runs the opposite direction: bending the product toward what the customer is actually doing. The FDE's most valuable output isn't code deployed. It's field signal that changes the roadmap.

Before — Deployment Frame
"My job is to make the product work at this customer."
After — Advocacy Frame
"My job is to make the product work for every customer like this one."
Every pattern you see at one customer is a leading indicator for every similar customer you haven't landed yet — and the product team will never see it unless you package it as roadmap evidence, not deployment trivia.

The Compounding Loop

Palantir built this loop explicitly. In their Q3 2024 earnings, they reported $725.5M in revenue with 30% year-over-year growth. Their US commercial segment hit $136M, up 54%. The company credits the forward deployed engineer model directly — not because FDEs are better at deploying software, but because each FDE's field discoveries get productized into the platform. An FDE finds a new use case at one account. That use case becomes a feature. That feature sells the next account without an FDE needing to rediscover it. The loop compounds.

Palantir's 10-K describes this as "our software improves as it is used" — but the mechanism isn't magical. It's FDEs turning observations into internal advocacy, consistently enough that product trusts the signal. The translation layer between field observation and roadmap item is the real infrastructure. Without it, you're just a deployment engineer with good instincts.

Crossing the Threshold

Databricks made a ~$1.3B bet on the same mechanism. Their solution architects and field engineers spent 2023 watching enterprise customers train LLMs outside Databricks — in separate ML tooling, with separate governance, creating the exact compliance and observability gaps the data platform was supposed to solve. The field signal was consistent: customers wanted training and inference inside the platform's governance boundary.

That signal drove the MosaicML acquisition in June 2023 and the DBRX model release in March 2024. Without field engineer advocacy, Databricks would have continued building standalone ML tooling. The field observation didn't just add a feature — it redirected a strategic investment. And it only landed because the signal crossed a credibility threshold: multiple accounts, same pattern, specific business impact. One anecdote doesn't redirect a billion-dollar roadmap. A pattern across accounts does.

When They Don't Want It

Confluent's field engineering team surfaced a different kind of signal in 2024. The product team assumed the main blocker for cloud migration was feature parity — that on-prem customers needed more capabilities before they'd switch. Field engineers heard something else. The blocker was organizational trust. Customers didn't doubt the platform's capabilities; they doubted their own ability to govern streaming data in a managed environment.

That signal shaped Confluent's investment in managed Apache Flink (GA in 2024) and tiered storage features — both of which addressed governance and control, not raw capability. The roadmap followed the field signal, not the internal assumption.

But notice the asymmetry. Confluent's field teams succeeded partly because "governance is the real blocker" was compatible with the cloud-first strategy. If the signal had been "customers actually want more on-prem features," the advocacy would have been harder. The credibility threshold would have been higher. The framing would have needed to be more precise. The hardest advocacy work is delivering signal that conflicts with internal conviction.

BEAT 2: Turn The Turn

Here's the turn most FDEs miss: your field notes are worth more to your own company than to your customer. Your customer pays you to solve their problem. Your company pays you to learn what the next hundred customers will need. Once you see that, it changes what you write down and who you send it to.

The Counterargument

"I'm not a product manager. My job is the engagement in front of me. Product has their own roadmap and their own data." True. Product does have their own roadmap, and it's usually built on different evidence than what you're seeing. That's exactly the point. Product teams optimize for signal they can measure at scale: usage telemetry, support tickets, sales pipeline data. You have signal they can't get: the work that happens before telemetry exists, before the customer is in the pipeline, before they've figured out how to articulate what they need.

That signal is worth more than telemetry because it's earlier. But early signal is weak signal. To override product's existing conviction, you need a pattern observed across enough engagements that it's undeniable. Palantir's loop works because the signal crosses a threshold of consistency — not because one FDE sent one Slack message.

What You Can Do

One Thing, Today

Write one field signal memo from your current or most recent engagement. Send it to one product counterpart by name. Not a Slack message — a short, direct document they can forward. If you don't know who to send it to, your first job is finding that person.
