What the Job Selects For

January 22, 2025

In December 2024, a Palantir Forward Deployed Software Engineer with three years in role posted on Blind that their RSUs — granted when the stock sat under $20 — were now worth over four times that after shares crossed $100. The post wasn't a celebration. It was a question: "I don't know if I'm actually good at this job or if I just got lucky on timing, and I have no way to evaluate that."

That question is the career problem. Comp, title, and exit opportunities are red herrings. It's the inability to self-assess because the job's selection criteria are invisible to the people being selected. Unlike a backend engineer, you can't point to a deploy and say "that's mine." Unlike a product engineer, you can't point to a metric and say "I moved that." The work lives in conversations, decisions not made, and code the customer's team eventually owns.

Perspective Shift

The FDE role is about building things for customers.

The FDE role is mostly about preventing things from being built.

The craft isn't construction — it's controlled demolition of bad requirements.

What Postings Miss

The FDE role doesn't select for people who are good with customers. It selects for people who can stand in a room full of stakeholders, know the thing they're all asking for is wrong, and say nothing — for three weeks.

Every engagement you've seen that "failed to launch" didn't fail because of bad code. It failed because someone talked too soon.

The Evidence

Palantir's Q3 2024 earnings highlighted 30% year-over-year revenue growth to $725 million, with management crediting expansion of AIP deployments — the customer-specific AI integration work that FDEs execute. When the stock surged past $100 in late December, up from under $20 nine months earlier, it created a comp event that masked craft development. If your total comp quadruples because of equity appreciation, you lose the price signal that normally tells you whether your skills are advancing. You can't calibrate.

Simultaneously, the role was fragmenting. Vercel posted "Frontier Engineer" positions in late 2024 — explicitly FDE-patterned work — specifying "experience deploying LLM applications in production with real customers." That requirement encodes a craft expectation: you have shipped something a non-technical end user touched, and it broke in a way you had to fix while they watched. A different filter than "strong communication skills," which is what most FDE-adjacent postings still lead with. Vercel wasn't alone in this window — multiple companies posted FDE-adjacent roles with different titles and subtly different expectations, but Vercel's posting stood out for making the production-deployment requirement explicit rather than burying it.

The comp data from this period makes the fragmentation concrete. On Levels.fyi, "Forward Deployed Engineer" roles at companies keeping that title showed total comp bands roughly $30–50K higher than "Solutions Engineer" or "Customer Engineer" roles at similarly staged companies, for job descriptions overlapping by roughly 70%. The premium didn't map to strategic importance or customer-facing scope. It mapped to production code responsibility — whether you were expected to commit code running in the customer's environment. A meaningful daily-work difference the JD blurs entirely. And that premium can vanish in a reorg when someone decides to "align titles across go-to-market."

Holding two incompatible mental models of a customer's problem simultaneously feels genuinely unpleasant — it's working memory operating near capacity, the same load you feel doing mental math while someone talks to you. The job trains you to live in that state for weeks. Not a superpower. A tolerance you build, like cold exposure.

The strongest counterargument is that this framing romanticizes inaction — that FDEs need decisiveness, and that failed engagements more often stem from analysis paralysis than premature commitment. But the Standish Group's CHAOS research through 2024 continues to show "incomplete requirements" and "lack of user involvement" as leading failure drivers, both downstream effects of committing to a technical direction before the problem space stabilizes. The restraint I'm describing isn't passivity. It's active information gathering that happens to look like patience.

How to Tell

  • This week, count how many customer meetings you left without stating your technical opinion. If zero, you're committing too early.
  • Wait for a customer to repeat a requirement in different words than they originally used. That's the abstraction settling.
  • Check your offer letter title, not the JD. Ask how long they expect you on a single engagement — the answer determines the daily experience.
  • Find one requirement you've mentally committed to. Write down the specific evidence that would make you abandon it. If you can't, you committed too early.
Perspective Shift

FDE career risk is about getting pigeonholed.

FDE career risk is about losing the ability to commit to a single technical direction because you've trained yourself out of it.

The skill that makes you good at the job — holding options open — becomes the thing that makes you bad at the next one.

After two or three years of deliberately not committing to a technical direction until the last responsible moment, you develop a reflexive aversion to commitment that doesn't turn off when you change roles. FDEs who move to staff engineering or architecture positions often struggle not because they lack technical depth, but because they keep generating options when the situation demands a decision. Someone asks "should we use Kafka or Redpanda?" and instead of picking one, you produce a four-page comparison. I've watched three former FDEs flounder in staff roles for exactly this reason. Smart people, strong technically, but unable to stop qualifying every recommendation with "on the other hand." The org reads it as lack of conviction. It's actually a trained reflex that served them well for years. The job trains a specific cognitive habit, and that habit has a half-life.

Within 24 hours

Find one requirement in your current engagement that you've already mentally committed to, and write down the specific piece of evidence that would make you abandon it. If you can't name that evidence, you committed too early.
