Not Yet Is the Deliverable

Field note · Customer-context judgment calls · research window 2025-08-01 to 2026-03-17

The Clean Room Problem

Week one of a new engagement usually looks the same. Someone from the customer's team has already built a demo — a Jupyter notebook, a Slack bot duct-taped to an internal wiki, something that answered three good questions in front of the VP and got the project greenlit. Now they want the FDE to make it real. The instinct, on both sides of the table, is to start typing. Nobody schedules a kickoff meeting to talk about what might be wrong with the plan.

A March 2026 survey of 650 enterprise technology leaders found that 78 percent of enterprises were running AI agent pilots, but only 14 percent had scaled one into organization-wide production. Five failure modes accounted for 89 percent of the projects that failed. The one this note is about runs underneath most of them: the difference between the curated data environment that made the pilot look good and the messy environment the system would actually have to run in.

That is not a confession. It is how demos get built. But it means a working prototype tells you almost nothing about whether the underlying problem is ready to be solved, and it means the FDE who inherits that prototype is inheriting a question, not an answer.

What Discovery Is For

This is the reframe, and it runs against how the job gets described in postings and career-advice threads: the value of an FDE is not the code they ship. It is the judgment call, made early and often under real pressure, to not ship yet — and to be able to say, specifically, what has to be true before they will.

At PostHog, this is not a personality trait some engineers happen to have. It is written into how the company runs FDE engagements: discovery and scoping is free and time-boxed, and if a customer wants more before signing, that becomes a separate, paid proof-of-concept. Every engagement needs a written outcome and a shared definition of done before delivery starts. A deliverable that grows mid-engagement is not handled with goodwill; it gets a new quote. Refusing to build is not an act of individual nerve you have to summon in the room. It is the default state, and building is what has to be earned.

[BEAT — AFTER] Once you see it that way, "don't build this yet" stops being a soft skill and starts being closer to an engineering gate, something with conditions attached, not a feeling you develop with enough scar tissue.

The FDEs trusted with the messiest engagements aren't the ones who say yes fastest. They're the ones whose "not yet" comes with a falsifiable reason attached.

The Case Against Waiting

None of this holds up if it just becomes an excuse to stall. The strongest counterargument is a real one: enterprise buying cycles are already slow, competitors move while you are scoping, and a customer who wanted a partner, not a consultant, can read caution as reluctance. Some FDEs have watched a careful discovery phase cost them the deal to a competitor who just built something, anything, and let the customer feel momentum in a way that a written scoping document never will.

[BEAT — TURN] The answer is not always wait. It is that the wait has to be time-boxed and diagnostic, not open-ended. MIT's Project NANDA studied more than 300 disclosed AI initiatives, plus dozens of structured interviews, and found that roughly 95 percent of enterprise generative AI pilots delivered no measurable financial return, while about 5 percent extracted real value. The split was not mainly about model quality. Organizations that bought from specialized vendors and built partnerships succeeded around 67 percent of the time; organizations that built alone succeeded roughly a third as often. The 5 percent were not the teams that moved fastest. They were the teams that had already answered the question a good discovery phase exists to ask, before a line of production code got written: does this system need to be built at all, or does it need to be bought, deferred, or aimed at a smaller target first.

That is the actual shape of the job people are hiring for right now, whether the posting says it or not, and it is why the interview loops for this role keep testing scoping instinct instead of algorithms.

What To Look For

If you are weighing whether this work fits you, or you are already in an engagement and something feels off, here is what the pattern actually looks like from the inside.

  • Get the "what has to be true in real data" answer in writing before touching an IDE.
  • Ask whether the demo's success depended on hand-picked inputs — if so, it proves nothing yet.
  • Treat mid-engagement scope growth as a new deliverable, not a favor.
  • Tie every addition back to the metric agreed on in discovery.

How This Fits The Arc

Do This Today

Pick one active or upcoming engagement. Before you write another line of code on it, write down the single sentence that says what has to be true in the customer's real, uncurated data for this build to be worth doing — and send it to your customer counterpart today, asking them to confirm or correct it. If you cannot write that sentence yet, that is the answer. You are still in discovery, and now you both know it.

Three things you'll pick up

"Don't build yet" can be a written policy, not a personal risk.
The pilot-to-production gap is a testable data condition.
The 95/5 split tracks how teams decided to build at all.
