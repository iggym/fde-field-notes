The Code You Don't Write

March 3, 2025 · 5 min read

On February 6th, Palantir reported full-year 2024 commercial revenue surpassing $1 billion. The number itself got attention. The mechanism behind it should get more.

That revenue was driven primarily by 2–5 day on-site sessions the company calls "bootcamps." A small team arrives with their AIP platform, connects it to the customer's actual data — not a sandbox, not sample records, the real production datasets — and by day three or four has working prototypes running against the customer's actual operational problems. The customer sees their own data moving through a working system. They sign the larger engagement.

The easy reading is "rapid prototyping wins deals." The harder reading, and the one that matters if you're trying to understand what FDE work actually is, is what the bootcamp produces. It isn't the engagement. It's the scoping phase, packaged and sold as its own thing. The prototypes are evidence supporting a judgment call, not deliverables. The actual output is a decision about what deserves to be built next, and what doesn't.

You probably think the value you bring to a customer engagement is the code you write.

Palantir's commercial growth engine — over a billion dollars in annual revenue — runs on sessions where the primary output is a judgment about what to build, not the build itself.

What you actually ship

Conventional: Ship working code Reframe: Ship a don't-build list

The real product an FDE ships is a "don't build" list. Everything else — prototypes, eval harnesses, integration spikes — is evidence for why each item belongs on it.

An FDE who ships twelve features but never once says "don't build this" has done less for that customer than one who ships three and kills nine.

Why prototypes lie to you

There's a named practice pattern in professional software delivery that makes this problem explicit. It's called a discovery spike: you time-box one to two weeks with a hard constraint that no production code ships. The only artifact you produce is a document answering two questions. What would we need to believe to build this? Do we actually believe it?

Practitioners using this pattern report that 40 to 60 percent of proposed features die in the spike. These are features that, without the spike, would have consumed engineering sprints, accumulated political commitment from stakeholders who saw them marked "in progress," and become exponentially harder to kill the further they progressed. Killing a feature after two sprints of development costs you technical debt and customer trust. Killing it after two days of structured thinking costs you a document.

I've watched this happen directly. A customer asked for an automated classification pipeline. We built a working version in two weeks. They loved the demo. Six weeks later they realized their manual process was faster and more accurate for their actual volume. We spent two months building something that made their operation worse. A two-day discovery spike would have caught it before a single line shipped.

The dynamic at work has a name from behavioral economics: escalating commitment. Once a customer sees a working prototype, they stop evaluating whether the underlying problem justifies a build. They start negotiating scope. The prototype has changed the conversation from "should we?" to "how much?" Palantir's bootcamp model works precisely because it front-loads the should-we-build-this-at-all question into the prototype phase itself, making the don't-build judgment part of the demo rather than a separate, uncomfortable conversation that happens after the customer has already started imagining the finished product.

The strongest counterargument is honest: some customers will perceive a "don't build" list as the vendor failing to deliver, regardless of how much value the avoidance creates. You recommended three things not to build — that's three things they wanted and aren't getting. The FDEs who handle this well don't present the list as "we're not building this." They present it as "here's what we learned doesn't fit your situation, and here's what we're building instead." The substance is identical. The reception is different.

Discovery

You're on-site. The customer has listed twelve things they want. Your job isn't to start building — it's to figure out which of those twelve should never be built. The output of this stage is a belief statement for each proposed feature: what would need to be true for this to be worth building?

Prototype

You've selected features to prototype based on the belief statements. The prototype is evidence, not a deliverable. It exists to test whether the beliefs hold up against real data.

Don't build this yet. The prototype just proved the underlying assumption is wrong. The customer's volume is too low. The latency budget doesn't support it. The data quality isn't there. This is the highest-value outcome of the entire engagement: you killed a feature before it cost engineering sprints.

The don't-build list grows here. That's the product you're actually shipping.

Eval Harness

For the features that survived discovery and prototype, you build an evaluation harness tuned to this customer's actual failure modes — not a generic benchmark. This is where technical craft and customer judgment fuse into something specific.

Productize

The surviving patterns become repeatable. The don't-build list from this engagement becomes input for the next one. Your scoping gets faster because you've accumulated real data about what doesn't work in this customer's domain.

How to actually do this

  • Present the don't-build list first. In your first scoping meeting, write down every feature request. Before leaving the room, identify which ones should not be built and why. Get the customer to react to it before they see anything working.
  • Insert a 48-hour belief-statement phase. For each proposed feature, write what you'd need to believe for it to be worth building, and whether you actually believe it. No code. Just claims on paper.
  • Treat "just build a quick version" as a scoping question. The response: "I can, but let me tell you what I'd need to believe about your situation for that to be the right thing to build."
  • Watch the customer's reaction to the don't-build list. If they push back hard, you haven't earned the trust yet. If they're curious about your reasoning, you have.

What to check tomorrow

Within 24 hours: find the last customer scoping document or kickoff summary you wrote. Count how many things it lists as "to build." Now write a second list: "things we considered and decided not to build." If that second list doesn't exist, or it's empty, that's your answer about where the leverage is.
