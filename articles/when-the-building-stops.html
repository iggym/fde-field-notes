When the Building Stops

March 18, 2025 Customer-Context Judgment

A Palantir forward-deployed engineer sat in a government agency conference room in early 2024, three weeks into what the contract called a "data integration sprint." Whiteboards covered every wall — boxes, arrows, labels in three different colors of marker. No code had been written. Not a line. The customer's deputy director was losing patience, the specific kind that shows up as forced cheerfulness and reheated questions about timeline. "When do we start actually building something?" The FDE pointed to the ontology map behind him, the web of data sources, decision authorities, and operational workflows, and said something close to: "We are building. Everything else depends on whether this is right."

That scene, described in WSJ reporting on Palantir's commercial practice, captures the uncomfortable center of customer-context judgment: the moment an FDE decides not to write code, knowing full well it will look like inaction to the person signing the checks. The pressure to produce visible output — a commit, a deploy, a dashboard — is enormous. Resisting it requires a specific kind of judgment that nobody posts about in job descriptions.

[BEAT 1] You've been told that saying "no" to a customer request is a relationship risk. The actual risk is building something that misses the real problem. The "no" isn't rejection. It's a redirect to the constraint that matters.

Not Technical, Structural

"Don't build this yet" isn't a technical judgment. It's an information-architecture check. You're not evaluating the proposed solution for technical soundness. You're evaluating whether the problem itself has enough structural integrity to support any solution at all.

A problem statement without structural integrity is like a foundation with no rebar — it holds shape until you load it, then it cracks along every line you didn't inspect.

Most engineers evaluate a proposed solution by asking: is this technically sound? The right question is different: is the problem specified tightly enough that "technically sound" even means anything here? A solution can be beautifully architected and still be wrong — not because the engineering was bad, but because there was nothing firm underneath it to anchor it to the customer's actual need.

What the Data Shows

McKinsey's 2024 State of Organizations survey — roughly 1,500 business leaders across industries — found that organizations reporting "clearly defined transformation objectives" were 2.4x more likely to report success than those without. Yet only 18% of respondents said their organization "consistently defined problem scope before committing to solution design." The gap isn't knowledge. Everyone knows you should define the problem first. The gap is discipline — the willingness to sit in the uncomfortable space of "we don't know enough yet" while a contract clock ticks.

The Standish Group's 2024 CHAOS Report adds sharper teeth. "Incomplete or poorly defined requirements" appeared in 49% of canceled projects — the single most-cited failure factor, ahead of budget overruns, technical debt, and team turnover. Projects where requirements were validated with end users before development started showed a 76% higher completion rate. That validation step isn't ceremonial. It's the load-bearing element that determines whether everything downstream can hold weight.

The pattern across both datasets is identical: the upfront work that determines whether a solution can succeed gets compressed or skipped entirely when delivery pressure mounts. The more visible the building, the more invisible the thinking becomes.

The obvious objection: this is just requirements engineering 101, repackaged as FDE insight. Nothing new. But the asymmetry in field deployments is materially different. In a normal product cycle, you own the rework loop — you catch the gap and fix it, then ship. In an FDE engagement, the customer's engineers start adapting their own systems — their pipelines, their operational playbooks, their downstream dependencies — around whatever you build. When it turns out to be wrong, the undo cost isn't just your engineering hours. It's their adapted code, their lost trust, their blown internal timeline. The rework penalty compounds because you don't control the blast radius.

[BEAT 2] You've been trained to assess whether to build by evaluating the technical merits of what was requested. Instead, assess whether a problem has been specified with enough constraints to bound a solution. Technical evaluation is premature until the problem statement passes a structural integrity check.
I've watched senior FDEs freeze in this exact moment — not because they couldn't see the gap, but because naming it out loud in front of the customer felt like admitting they'd wasted three weeks. They hadn't. But the feeling was real.

What to Actually Do

Watch for the silence. The customer describes a solution — "we need a pipeline that does X" — and their own engineers go quiet. That silence is data. It means the customer's team sees the gap between what's being requested and what's actually understood, but nobody in the room has social permission to name it. The senior engineer won't contradict the director with a vendor watching. A junior engineer won't speak first. Somewhere, a product manager is calculating the political capital cost of a pushback. Your job as the outside engineer — the person with the least organizational baggage in the room — is to have less to lose and more obligation to speak. Name the gap. Don't start coding.

Check constraint density. A problem statement with structural integrity rules out most possible solutions. If you can imagine three materially different technical approaches that all seem valid given what you've heard, the problem isn't specified yet. Keep pulling on the thread until the constraints narrow the field.

Produce a visible artifact from your scoping work. Palantir's ontology mapping works partly because it generates a diagram — something the customer can point to in their own internal meetings. "We spent three weeks on this" lands differently when there's a document on the table. If your problem-definition work lives only in your head or your private notes, you look idle. And the customer has no way to defend the time you spent to their own leadership.

When you get this right, something shifts in the room. The customer's engineers — the ones who went silent — start talking. They'll confirm what you said, sometimes eagerly, sometimes cautiously. That's your confirmation signal. You haven't just delayed building. You've given the customer's own team permission to do the work they already knew needed doing.

  • Watch for the silence when a customer describes a solution
  • Check constraint density — can you rule out most solutions?
  • Produce a visible artifact from your scoping work

In your next customer conversation, count how many times the customer describes a solution versus describes a problem. If the ratio exceeds 3:1, stop building. Open a blank document. Start mapping constraints.
