The Title Isn't the Job

An "FDE" title doesn't describe a role — it describes a company's bet about how software gets sold.

A friend sent me two offer letters last month. One said "Forward Deployed Engineer" at a Series B infrastructure startup. The other: "Solutions Engineer" at a public data company. Base salary: $165K and $170K. Equity: unvested options at a 2x preference stack versus RSUs with a known strike price. Both led their descriptions with "customer-facing technical work." Each one used the word "bespoke." She asked which one to take.

I told her the title was the wrong variable to compare. Titles matter — but in a direction she wasn't looking.

An "FDE" title doesn't describe a role. It describes a company's bet about how software gets sold. When you see that label on a posting, you're not reading a job description. You're reading how the company has positioned technical labor inside its go-to-market motion — and whether that position makes what you build durable or disposable.

What the Data Shows

Levels.fyi's compensation data through early 2025 reveals something most candidates don't expect: "Forward Deployed Engineer," "Solutions Engineer," "Customer Engineer," and "Field Engineer" roles share roughly 70% comp-band overlap at equivalent experience levels. A mid-level FDE at Palantir and a mid-level Solutions Engineer at Snowflake sit in the same $190K–$250K total-comp corridor. A Customer Engineer at Google overlaps both bands from below and above. The label, as a price signal, is nearly useless.

The confusion makes more sense when you look at where the title came from. Palantir originated "Forward Deployed Engineer" and structured its FDEs under engineering, not sales. Their 2024 SEC filings and public job descriptions frame the role consistently: product engineers who deploy on customer sites, building forward rather than selling sideways. The comp structure matches — equity-heavy, with vesting tied to deployment milestones rather than revenue quota. The original FDE was, structurally, a product engineer with a customer-shaped context.

By late 2024, the title had scattered across the industry with its structural meaning stripped out. Replicate posted "Field Engineer" roles at $160K–$210K total comp — more base salary, less equity, different incentive structure than Palantir's model. Vercel listed similar positions under "Developer Experience" rather than a dedicated field organization. Same rough title tier, four different organizational homes, four different definitions of what the person in the seat is actually accountable for.

Here's the pattern that emerged: the strongest signal in the title noise isn't compensation. It's whether the company has bothered to differentiate leveling for the role. Organizations posting a single undifferentiated "FDE" title — no I, II, Senior, Staff — often haven't built a career path for the role, regardless of what the hiring manager verbally promises during interviews. Databricks and Snowflake, which use "Solutions Engineer" with explicit level hierarchies and published ranges, have made an organizational commitment to the role as a long-term career track. A startup posting one flat "FDE" title has made a different bet, even if they don't know it.

If the pay is roughly the same, what isn't the same is whether the company has built a structure for you to grow in.

The obvious pushback: comp does matter, and a $30K gap at the same title is a real signal about how the company values the work. That's fair — but that gap almost always maps to something structural underneath (equity versus cash split, reporting line, revenue attribution model) that the title alone won't show you. You need to see the structure behind the number, not just the number itself.

I've made this mistake. I once compared two "FDE" offers by comp alone and took the higher one. The higher-paying role reported into sales, had no leveling, and I was doing solution-selling demos within six months. The lower-paying one — which a colleague took — had a Staff-level FDE ladder and real engineering influence. The $20K difference cost me two years of career trajectory.

What to Actually Do

Within 24 hours, find one person who currently holds the exact title at the company you're evaluating and ask them what organization they report into. That single data point will reframe your entire decision.
