You're Not Deploying, You're Correcting

Feb 18, 2025 Internal Advocacy

You land at a mid-size financial services firm for what's scoped as a standard two-week deployment. By day three, you notice something the statement of work never mentioned: the customer's analysts keep building the same retrieval-augmented generation pipeline, step by step, engagement after engagement. Connect to their internal document store. Chunk the text. Embed it. Surface it to an LLM. Check for hallucinations. Retry on failure. You've watched three separate customer teams in the last month arrive at this exact pattern independently — none of them knew the others existed, and all of them spent four to five engineering days on plumbing that should have been a configured option.

You file a ticket. Not a feature request — a pattern observation, with specific evidence of convergence. Two sprints later, it ships as a first-class feature. Within the following quarter, that template gets used in forty or more deployments across the customer base.

Palantir's Q3 2024 earnings call credited precisely this dynamic — field-identified patterns accelerating from one-off manual builds to productized features — as a meaningful driver of their roughly 30% year-over-year commercial revenue growth. The bootcamp model their forward-deployed engineers run at customer sites isn't primarily a deployment strategy. It's a sensing strategy, and the product organization is the receiver.1Before: "My job is to make this deployment succeed."
After: "My job is to make it succeed and ensure the next FDE doesn't have to solve this manually."
Turn: A deployment you hand-built is a product failure you failed to report.

Your highest-output work isn't what you ship to the customer. It's what you ship back to your own product team.
A forward-deployed engineer who only deploys is a sensor that never transmits.

The thing most FDEs miss about internal advocacy: the signal has a half-life. Snowflake's field teams throughout 2024 kept encountering the same refrain from customers across industries — they wanted to run AI models against data already sitting in Snowflake, not extract it and ship it to a separate AI platform. Internal product direction had initially assumed the opposite trajectory: that customers would move their data to where the specialized models lived. The field signal contradicted the product assumption directly.

In Snowflake's Q3 FY2025 earnings call, CEO Sridhar Ramaswamy discussed how field-driven insights reshaped the Cortex AI strategy toward a bring-models-to-the-data architecture. The correction didn't originate in a quarterly business review or a customer advisory board. It came from field engineers escalating a pattern while it was still forming — before the product team committed engineering resources to the wrong direction. Once the internal assumption was locked into a sprint plan, reversing it would have cost weeks. Field engineers at other companies who sat on similar observations — waiting for the "right time" to escalate — watched their product teams commit to architectures that customers then rejected. The cost wasn't abstract. It was measured in failed proof-of-concepts and lost deals.

I've watched FDEs file meticulous post-engagement documents that nobody reads. The timing kills the signal every time. You filed it two weeks after the customer signed off; by then the product team is three sprints into different priorities. Your carefully written observation has become historical fiction — accurate, but irrelevant.

+ "But we already have feedback loops."

Those channels exist in most mature organizations. But they filter field reality through at least two translation layers before it reaches someone who can build a fix. The customer success manager summarizes what they heard. The product manager prioritizes based on a portfolio of such summaries. By arrival, the signal has been sanitized into a feature request stripped of the context that made it urgent. The FDE is the only role that observes the problem while holding the keyboard. That proximity produces a different class of data — not what customers say they want in a structured feedback session, but what their hands actually do when nobody's watching.

The transmitter is broken by default in most FDE organizations. The observations exist. The plumbing doesn't.2Before: "I'll write up my findings after the engagement wraps."
After: "If I'm not feeding signals during the engagement, I'm already too late."
Turn: Advocacy is a sprint-level practice, not a post-mortem.

Should You Escalate Now?

So What Changes

  • ✓ File the pattern, not the request. One customer asking for a feature is a support ticket. Three customers independently converging on the same workaround is a roadmap signal. When you escalate, lead with the convergence — the fact that separate teams arrived at identical solutions without coordination — not the feature itself. Convergence is evidence of a real gap. A single request is an anecdote.
  • ✓ Escalate in the same sprint you observe it. Create a standing channel — a Slack thread, a shared document, a weekly fifteen-minute sync — where raw field observations land while the engagement is still live. If your organization doesn't have one, that's the first thing you build. You're holding the observations. You're also the person best positioned to notice that the plumbing is missing.
  • ✓ Name the product failure explicitly. When you hand-built something that should be a product feature, frame it that way: "I had to construct X manually because the product doesn't support it. Three separate customers required this. Here's why." That framing — gap, not enhancement — changes how product engineers process the information. A gap implies something is missing and causing active friction. An enhancement implies something could be nicer. Friction gets fixed. Nice-to-haves get backlogged.

You'll know the plumbing is working when a product engineer messages you mid-engagement asking for more detail — not when you submit a post-mortem that gets a thumbs-up emoji.

Do This Tonight

Within the next 24 hours: Identify one thing you manually built or worked around on your most recent engagement that should exist as a product feature. Write exactly three sentences describing the gap. Send it to a product engineer — not a PM, an engineer who would build the fix. Observe what comes back.
