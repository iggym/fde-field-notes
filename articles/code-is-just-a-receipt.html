Code Is Just a Receipt

March 8, 2025 Discovery & Scoping 5 min read

Editor's note (Oct 2026): Revenue figures were corrected against Palantir's Q4 2024 release. The bootcamp conversion figure was revised from an unsourced ~80% to the ~75% that analyst estimates report; Palantir has not published an official rate. The Snowflake and Salesforce executive remarks have not been independently verified and are under review.

On February 3rd, Palantir reported $702 million in US commercial revenue for 2024, up 54 percent. On the earnings call, the number that should have stopped every forward-deployed engineer in the room wasn't the revenue. It was the bootcamp conversion rate. Palantir's AIP Bootcamps — those multi-day sessions where FDEs show up at a customer site and work alongside their team — convert prospects to contracts at roughly three in four, according to analyst estimates; Palantir itself hasn't published an official rate. For enterprise software, where typical pilot-to-production conversion languishes far lower, that figure is remarkable. The gap isn't a technology advantage. It's a process advantage, and the process is almost entirely discovery. The kind where you bring their data to the table and start finding out what's actually broken, rather than taking requests.

Here's what actually happens in those bootcamps, based on what the company has described publicly and what practitioners have corroborated: FDEs walk in with no pre-built demo. They ask the customer for access to their own data. Over three to five days, they work side-by-side with the customer's engineers and domain experts to identify three to five use cases that could run on the customer's actual infrastructure with the customer's actual data. Then they build a rapid prototype of the highest-priority one. Before the bootcamp, there's a vague sense of what might be useful. After it, there's a prioritized, validated backlog with a working prototype on real data and a room full of customer stakeholders who've personally touched it.

The code they write during those three to five days isn't the point. It's evidence that the scoping was right.

[BEAT 1] BEFORE: Discovery is what you do before the real work starts. AFTER: Discovery is the work. Everything after is just execution against a judgment call you already made. TURN: Palantir's bootcamps convert as well as they reportedly do because they treat the scoping session as the deliverable, not the setup for one.

In forward-deployed engineering, the discovery phase isn't a prelude to the engagement. It IS the engagement. The code that follows is just the receipt.

A surgeon doesn't start cutting to figure out where the problem is. Neither should you.

The evidence that this distinction is hard — and that most organizations get it wrong — showed up twice in the same week in late February. On Snowflake's earnings call on February 26th, the telling moment came when CEO Sridhar Ramaswamy explained they were restructuring field technical teams to separate "solution design and discovery" from "implementation delivery." One of the largest data infrastructure companies on earth concluded, on a public earnings call, that the people good at scoping are not the same people good at building, and that pretending otherwise was actively hurting customer outcomes. They didn't say it gently. They said it as a structural problem requiring an org-chart fix. What that looks like in practice: implementation engineers who were being asked to also run discovery sessions were defaulting to "build what the customer asked for" because they didn't have the context or the mandate to push back on bad requests.

Same day, different call. Salesforce reported $9.99 billion in quarterly revenue. Marc Benioff discussed Agentforce AI agent deployments and noted, with unusual directness, that scoping sessions for AI use cases were "more complex than traditional implementations" and were straining professional services capacity. The old discovery model — sit down, map the customer's requirements to your feature set, build it — breaks completely when the customer doesn't know what's possible with AI and you don't know what decisions they're making badly. You can't map requirements to features when the requirements are really just unexamined judgment calls. A customer asking for "an AI agent that handles customer support" hasn't done any discovery. They've named a product category. The FDE's job in that moment is to figure out which specific decisions the customer's support team is making badly, and whether an agent can actually improve them.

The strongest counterargument here is straightforward: if discovery is the deliverable, why do customers pay for implementation at all? Because scoping judgment is necessary but not sufficient. Someone still has to build the thing, validate it against the customer's actual failure modes, and hand it off sustainably. Discovery doesn't replace implementation. It makes implementation possible instead of speculative. Skip it, and you get the pattern every FDE has seen: a polished prototype the customer loved in the demo, abandoned three months later because it was scoped to a hallucinated problem.

[BEAT 2] BEFORE: If I'm not writing code, I'm not adding value. AFTER: The code is the receipt. The judgment embedded in the scoping is the value. TURN: Snowflake had to restructure their entire field organization around the realization that asking engineers who are good at building to also be good at discovering is like asking a surgeon to also run diagnostic imaging.
Working memory holds roughly four chunks of novel information at once. When you walk into a discovery session with twenty unsorted customer requests and no framework for prioritizing them, you've already exceeded cognitive capacity before you've started. Structure the input, or your brain will silently drop half of it.

How to Tell

Scoping quality checklist

Spend the first half-day refusing to open an IDE. Write down every assumption the customer has, then ask which ones they've tested against real data. Most haven't tested any. That list of untested assumptions is your real deliverable.
When a customer says "we need a dashboard for X," ask "what decision will someone make differently after looking at this?" If they can't answer in one sentence, the scope problem isn't the dashboard — it's that they haven't identified the decision.
Track what percentage of your engagement time is pre-commit. Under 25% means you're almost certainly under-scoping. Palantir's bootcamps invert the ratio — the majority of time is pre-code. That's the point.
If you finish discovery and the customer's original request list looks exactly the same as when you walked in, you didn't do discovery. You took notes.

Within 24 hours: find the last engagement you shipped. Open your notes from day one. Count how many of the customer's original requests you actually built versus how many you reframed, combined, or declined. If the ratio is above 80 percent built-as-requested, you didn't do discovery. You took orders.
