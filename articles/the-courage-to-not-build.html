Field Note — January 6, 2026

The Courage to Not Build

Customer-context judgment calls and the "don't build this yet" moments that separate trusted FDEs from liabilities.

📅 Jan 6, 2026 🔬 Research window: Jan 2025 – Jan 2026 🏷️ Judgment & Trust

In July 2025, MIT's Project NANDA published a finding that should have changed how every FDE starts their day. After interviewing 150 leaders, surveying 350 employees, and analyzing 300 public AI deployments, the researchers concluded that 95% of enterprise generative AI pilots delivered zero measurable P&L impact. Not low impact. Zero. The $30–40 billion enterprises had poured into these initiatives was, by the study's own accounting, largely unaccounted for in any profit-and-loss statement.

A manufacturing COO told the researchers something quieter and more devastating than the headline: "The hype on LinkedIn says everything has changed, but in our operations, nothing fundamental has shifted." That sentence is the FDE's briefing. Not a technical spec. A statement about organizational reality.

Beat 1 — Before / Turn / After

Your Job Is to Stop

Most engineers enter this role believing their value is measured in shipped code. The posting you answered promised production-grade solutions. Your interview tested system design, data modeling, and customer communication. Quarterly reviews reward only deployment velocity. Every signal in your environment points toward building.

Here is the turn. Your highest-leverage act is often refusing to build. When a customer's data lives in three silos with no named owner, when the compliance review has not started, when the success metric is "make leadership happy"—the correct engineering decision is to halt. Not to slow down. Stop, name the blocker, and refuse to accumulate sunk cost.

Companies hire FDEs for their ability to ship. They retain the great ones for their ability to recognize when shipping is the wrong move. The skill that separates a trusted FDE from a liability is not technical depth; it is the willingness to be disliked for twelve minutes in a conference room.

The FDE who can read the pre-build signals prevents their company from becoming another line in next year's failure statistics.

The Numbers Behind the No

The MIT finding is not an outlier. RAND Corporation interviewed 65 experienced AI practitioners and found that more than 80% of AI projects fail, with the rate roughly double that of non-AI IT projects. The highest-impact failure cause was not technical. It was misaligned incentives and the absence of end-user co-design. S&P Global Market Intelligence reported in 2025 that 42% of U.S. companies had abandoned most of their AI initiatives, up from 17% the prior year. Gartner predicted 60% of AI projects lacking AI-ready data would be abandoned through 2026.

These numbers describe a market drowning in pilots that should never have been approved. The FDE who sees the pre-build signals—data fragmented across silos, no named owner with decision authority, success metrics defined after sprint one—prevents their company from becoming another line in next year's failure statistics.

Honest aside

I have never met an FDE who regretted stopping a bad-fit engagement early. I have met many who regretted not stopping one. The regret curve is asymmetric. The twelve minutes of discomfort in the room are cheaper than six months of explaining why the perfectly functional model nobody uses is not their fault.

How Palantir Filters Before Building

Palantir understood this structurally. Their bootcamp model, which helped drive U.S. commercial revenue up 137% year-over-year by Q4 2025, compresses the discovery phase into one to five days of working directly on the customer's data and actual operational problems. The bootcamp is not a sales tactic. It is a filtering mechanism. If the customer's data cannot support a functioning capability in five days, the engagement is scoped differently—or not at all.

Mark Coleman, co-founder of NetBox Labs, captured the FDE's actual function when he told The New Stack: "People don't know what they want until they see something they don't want." The FDE's value is not in delivering the perfect solution on the first attempt. It is in running the cheap experiment that reveals the real requirement.

Beat 2 — Before / Turn / After

Failure Wears a Different Face

Before you have watched a few engagements die, failure looks like a broken model. Accuracy drops. Latency spikes. The demo crashes. These are visible, fixable, and almost never the real reason a project ends.

The turn comes when you realize that failure usually looks like a perfectly working model that nobody uses. MIT found that internal AI builds succeed roughly a third as often as purchased solutions. The difference is not the technology. It is the presence of an external voice willing to name the friction that internal teams are politically constrained to ignore.

After this shift, you stop judging readiness by technical feasibility. You judge it by organizational friction. Can one person decide? Does the data have an owner? Has the workflow been observed, not described? These are the gates that matter.

Shadow AI Is a Diagnostic

MIT's report documented what it called a "shadow AI economy." Even when official pilots fail, 90% of employees use personal AI tools for job tasks. At one Fortune 500 insurer, the sanctioned GenAI pilot collapsed because it could not retain context, while employees quietly relied on personal tools to speed claims processing.

Most organizations treat shadow AI as a governance threat. The practiced FDE treats it as a map. Shadow adoption reveals where real workflow demand lives. Before you design the official solution, map the shadow usage. It will tell you more about what the customer actually needs than any requirements document.

Four Practices to Adopt

If you are early in this career, or considering it, here is what to practice.

  • Run a five-minute data audit before agreeing to any scope. Ask: what system holds the data, who owns it, and when did it last change?
  • Define the "no" in writing before sprint one. Document what would make you recommend stopping. Show it to the customer.
  • Map shadow AI usage before designing the official solution. It reveals real workflow demand.
  • Calibrate your "no" to the customer's maturity. A startup with clean data can absorb a fast build. A siloed enterprise cannot.

The Strongest Counterargument

The obvious objection is that saying "no" loses deals. In a market where OpenAI's FDE team grew from two engineers to ten-plus across eight cities in under two years, and where job postings for the role increased 800% between January and September 2025, the pressure to say yes is enormous.

But the MIT data cuts the other way. Vendor-led projects succeed roughly 67% of the time, while internal builds succeed only about 33%. The difference is not the technology. It is the presence of an external voice willing to name the friction that internal teams are politically constrained to ignore. The FDE who says "not yet" is not losing the deal. They are preserving the relationship for the moment when the customer is actually ready to succeed.
